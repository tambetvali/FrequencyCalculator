# How log and exp convert to gaussian and fourier

Linear axe ***X***: octave 0 = differential / integral 0.

Exponent axe ***Y***: octave 1 = integral 1.

Linear axe ***Z***: octave -1 = differential 1.

If you create linear function at octave 0, the same function from different frequencies:
- Appears exponent in Y, int 1.
- Appears logarithm at Z, diff 1.

The way these functions connect:
- One can see, through all infinity the density of exp compared to lin becomes more and more dense: at the end, last points of exp contain all the points while lin is keeping stable number of points. The direction is bigger and numbers are bigger: at next octave, they start again from complexity-reduced positions, Y applied before their initial ones.
- Log at diff1 will be less and less dense at the end, adding "vacumutesimals" between each number, while it will be denser and denser at area below 0 and especially minus infinity; we can see the future, altough points are progressing, is more and more work for equal progress and thus, in infinity the truth fades off and at exact infinity, the log continues at sub-zero, under-threshold line.

Fourier decomposition of octavian, triangle-ended numbers:
- For lin space, at log space each frequency of a point is separate component on it's local slice, distributing frequencies in this octave-speedly growing number.
- Frequencies become separate components, size-aligned to being locally same range as the whole number.

Gaussian composition:
- For lin space, at exp space the first frequential component now combines all frequencies of the lower space and curve can be expressed as single number, where gaussian composition and fourier discomposition become the opposites.
- Line partials become single component, pointing to single frequential number size-aligned; the rest of components can determine the long-term outcome in this number space or depending on unit conversion, represent various important attributes.
